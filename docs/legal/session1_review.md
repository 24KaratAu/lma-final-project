A general point before the detail. Sections 27 and 28 both say the agreement is void "to that extent". A clause can be partly void and partly good, and the rest of the lease is unaffected. Decide now how you will label a clause where only one sentence offends. My suggestion: label it void, and use the reason column to say which words are void.

## 1. The three kinds of clause

The three categories are right, and they cover the ground for these two sections.

**Restraint of trade (s.27).** Your example ("shall not run a similar business within 5 km for 3 years after the lease ends") is void. Under Indian law it does not matter that the restraint is limited in area and time. Unlike English law, s.27 has no reasonableness test outside the goodwill exception. A partial restraint is as void as a total one (*Madhub Chunder*), and a restraint operating after the contract ends is void however reasonable it looks (*Krishan Murgai*, *Percept D'Mark*).

**Absolute bar (s.28(a), first limb).** Your example ("landlord's decision on any dispute is final and cannot be challenged in any court") is void. One party is made judge of its own cause and the courts are shut out entirely.

Be careful with a related pattern you will see in real leases: a clause making the decision of an architect, surveyor or valuer "final and binding" on a technical question such as measurement of area, or a certificate of completion of fit-outs. That is closer to an agreed method of fixing a fact than an ouster of the courts. I would mark those **refer** rather than void, unless the clause also says expressly that no court may look at the matter.

**Time limits (s.28(a), second limb, and s.28(b)).** Your example ("barred unless a suit is filed within 3 months") is void under s.28(a), because it limits the time within which the tenant may enforce their rights.

On the history, because it affects older leases: clause (b) was added by the Indian Contract (Amendment) Act, 1996, in force from 8 January 1997. Before that, the courts drew a line between a clause limiting the time to sue (void) and a clause saying the right itself ends after a period (valid). See *Sujir Ganesh Nayak* and *Food Corporation of India v New India Assurance*. Clause (b) removed that distinction. The Supreme Court has held that the amendment is not retrospective (*Union of India v Indusind Bank*). All five pilot leases are dated after 1997, so this should not bite, but record the execution date of every lease you use. If any deed is from before 1997, an extinguishment clause in it is valid.

For the "section" column, I would record:
- s.28(a) where the clause speaks of the time to sue or bring a claim, and
- s.28(b) where it says the right is extinguished, waived or forfeited, or the other side is discharged, once a period expires.

## 2. The look-alikes

**"During the lease, the tenant shall use the premises only as an office."** Valid. This is a user clause restricting what can be done on the landlord's property. It does not restrain the tenant from carrying on any trade elsewhere. Every commercial lease has one, so it is a good look-alike.

**"Any dispute shall be referred to arbitration under the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996."** Valid under Exception 1 to s.28. Three points for when you write variants:

- Disputes between landlord and tenant under the Transfer of Property Act are arbitrable (*Vidya Drolia*, which overruled *Himangni Enterprises* on this point). Tenancies governed by a rent control statute are not, because those go to the forum the statute creates. Your scope note already sends rent control to "refer", which is right.
- The clause must actually be an agreement to arbitrate. "Disputes **may** be referred to arbitration" is not one (*Jagdish Chander*). If a clause says disputes may go to arbitration and also bars the courts, Exception 1 does not save it, and it should be read as an absolute bar. That makes a good hard item.
- A clause letting the landlord alone appoint a sole arbitrator is still an arbitration agreement, so it is valid for s.28 purposes. Only the appointment procedure is bad (*TRF*, *Perkins Eastman*, and the Constitution Bench in *CORE*). Label it valid, and mention the appointment point in the reason column if you like.

**"The tenant shall notify the landlord of any defect within 30 days."** Valid as written. It is a step under the contract and says nothing about going to court. But it becomes a different clause the moment you add a consequence: "failing which the tenant shall be deemed to have waived all claims in respect of the defect", or "and the landlord shall have no liability thereafter". That version extinguishes a right, or discharges a party from liability, on the expiry of a specified period, so it falls under s.28(b) and is void. I would put both versions in the item pool. The pair tests exactly the distinction your checklist is built on.

## 3. Comments on the checklist

### Restraint of trade (s.27)

The fact list is good. Changes I would make:

- **"Starting when, and for how long"** decides nothing in a void/valid sense, because duration does not save a post-lease restraint. Keep recording it, but note that it only matters under Exception 1.
- **Goodwill.** Exception 1 is narrower than your rule suggests. It applies only where the restraint is (i) given by the seller of goodwill to the buyer, (ii) within specified local limits, and (iii) only so long as the buyer, or someone deriving title from them, carries on a like business there. If goodwill is sold but the clause has no local limit, it falls outside the exception and is void. My suggested rule: goodwill sold and all three conditions met → **refer** (reasonableness of the limits is for the court, as you say). Goodwill sold but a condition missing → **void**. In practice you will rarely see goodwill in an office lease. It turns up in leases of running hotels, restaurants and shops.
- **Restraints on the landlord.** Leases sometimes say the landlord will not let other units in the building to the tenant's competitors during the term. That is a restriction on how the landlord deals with its own property, operating only during the term, and I would treat it as valid. If it is drafted to continue after the lease ends, mark it refer.

**Question 1: restraint on a similar business elsewhere, during the lease only.**

The starting point is that a negative covenant operating only during the contract is generally not a restraint of trade under s.27 (*Niranjan Shankar Golikari*, *Gujarat Bottling*, and *Percept D'Mark*, which restated the point). But those cases concern employment and franchise contracts, where the employer or franchisor has a real interest in the other party's exclusive service. *Gujarat Bottling* also keeps open the possibility that an in-term restraint is void if it is unconscionable, excessively harsh or one-sided.

A landlord usually has no comparable interest in what the tenant does elsewhere. The exception is a retail lease where rent is a share of turnover: a radius clause then protects the landlord's income, and that is the commercial justification courts would look at.

So my answer:
- in-term restraint confined to the premises or the building: **valid**;
- in-term restraint on carrying on business elsewhere: **refer**, because whether it is saved depends on the commercial context, which the text alone will not settle;
- any restraint continuing after the lease ends: **void**, unless Exception 1 applies.

### Absolute bar on going to court (s.28(a), first limb)

The rule is right. Parties may choose one of several courts that already have jurisdiction (*Hakam Singh*, *A.B.C. Laminart*, *Swastik Gases*). They cannot give jurisdiction to a court that has none.

One refinement will cut down your "refer" pile. For a lease, the court where the premises are situated always has jurisdiction over a suit about the property itself, such as possession (s.16 CPC). The lease tells you where the premises are. So a clause naming the courts of the city where the premises are located is **valid** without more. The genuinely doubtful case is a clause naming courts in some other city (often the landlord's registered office), because a money claim may well be maintainable there under s.20 CPC, depending on where the parties reside and where the cause of action arose. Keep **refer** for that case only.

Add "is there an arbitration clause elsewhere in the lease?" to the facts. An absolute-sounding finality clause has to be read together with any arbitration clause, and the two often sit far apart in the deed.

### Time limits (s.28(a) second limb, and s.28(b))

The rule is right, with one correction on bank guarantees. Exception 3 saves a term stipulated by a bank or financial institution in a guarantee, and only where the period is at least one year from the specified event. A clause in the lease itself, between landlord and tenant, is not a bank's contract, even if it is about a bank guarantee the tenant must furnish. Exception 3 will therefore almost never decide a lease clause. My suggestion:
- clause in the lease between the parties → apply the ordinary rule;
- the guarantee itself, period of one year or more → valid;
- the guarantee itself, period under one year → void;
- the period cannot be worked out → refer.

**Question 2: periods shorter than the statutory limitation period, and the period for lease claims.**

Yes. A clause shortening the time within which a party may sue is void to that extent, however short or long the cut. A clause that matches or exceeds the statutory period is not void under s.28, but it gives no extra time either: limitation under the Limitation Act, 1963 runs regardless of what the parties agree (s.3). For labelling, treat a period equal to or longer than the statutory one as **valid** (no effect on the right to sue), and anything shorter as **void**.

The usual periods under the Schedule to the Limitation Act, 1963 for commercial lease claims are:

| Claim | Article | Period | Runs from |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrears of rent | 52 | 3 years | when the arrears become due |
| Damages for breach of the lease (either side) | 55 | 3 years | when the contract is broken (or, for successive or continuing breaches, as the Article provides) |
| Specific performance (e.g. an option to renew) | 54 | 3 years | the date fixed for performance, or when performance is refused |
| Landlord's suit to recover possession from a tenant | 67 | 12 years | when the tenancy is determined |
| Refund of a security deposit, and anything not specially provided for | 55 or 113 | 3 years | breach, or when the right to sue accrues |

In short, three years for money claims and twelve years for the landlord's possession suit. The Limitation Act also applies to arbitrations (s.43 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act), so a short period inside an arbitration clause is void in the same way.

## 4. Outside scope

Your list is sensible. Two additions you will meet in real leases:

- **Caps and exclusions of liability** ("the landlord shall not be liable for any loss beyond one month's rent"). These limit what can be recovered, not the right to go to court, so they are not s.28 clauses. Mark them outside scope, not void.
- **Waiver of the right to an injunction** ("the tenant's only remedy shall be damages"). Same reasoning: refer, not s.28.

## 5. Leading judgments

Every citation below should be checked against the reported text before anyone relies on it. Your plan already requires a team member to read each judgment before it goes in, which is the right approach.

**Section 27**

1. *Madhub Chunder Poramanick v Rajcoomar Doss* (1874) 14 Beng LR 76: partial restraints are void too; no reasonableness test outside the statutory exception.
2. *Brahmaputra Tea Co Ltd v Scarth* (1885) ILR 11 Cal 545: covenant operating during service upheld; the part operating after it ended held void.
3. *Niranjan Shankar Golikari v Century Spinning and Manufacturing Co Ltd* AIR 1967 SC 1098: a negative covenant operating during the term of the contract is not a restraint of trade.
4. *Superintendence Company of India (P) Ltd v Krishan Murgai* (1981) 2 SCC 246: restraint operating after the contract ends is void under s.27.
5. *Gujarat Bottling Co Ltd v Coca Cola Co* (1995) 5 SCC 545: negative stipulation during a franchise term not void, unless unconscionable, excessively harsh or one-sided.
6. *Percept D'Mark (India) (P) Ltd v Zaheer Khan* (2006) 4 SCC 227: restrictive covenant extending beyond the term is void; reasonableness is not the test under s.27.

**Section 28: choice of court**

7. *Hakam Singh v Gammon (India) Ltd* (1971) 1 SCC 286: parties may choose one of several competent courts; this does not offend s.28.
8. *A.B.C. Laminart (P) Ltd v A.P. Agencies* (1989) 2 SCC 163: same, and parties cannot confer jurisdiction on a court that has none.
9. *Swastik Gases (P) Ltd v Indian Oil Corporation Ltd* (2013) 9 SCC 32: exclusive jurisdiction clause read as excluding other competent courts even without words like "only".

**Section 28: time limits**

10. *Food Corporation of India v New India Assurance Co Ltd* (1994) 3 SCC 324: pre-amendment distinction between limiting time to sue and extinguishing the right.
11. *National Insurance Co Ltd v Sujir Ganesh Nayak & Co* (1997) 4 SCC 366: same distinction, applied to an insurance policy.
12. *Union of India v Indusind Bank Ltd* (2016) 9 SCC 720: the 1997 amendment to s.28 is not retrospective.

**Section 28: arbitration (Exception 1)**

13. *K.K. Modi v K.N. Modi* (1998) 3 SCC 573: the attributes of an arbitration agreement, as distinct from an expert determination.
14. *Jagdish Chander v Ramesh Chander* (2007) 5 SCC 719: "may be referred to arbitration" is not a binding arbitration agreement.
15. *Vidya Drolia v Durga Trading Corporation* (2021) 2 SCC 1: landlord–tenant disputes under the Transfer of Property Act are arbitrable; rent control matters are not.
16. *TRF Ltd v Energo Engineering Projects Ltd* (2017) 8 SCC 377: a person ineligible to be an arbitrator cannot appoint one.
17. *Perkins Eastman Architects DPC v HSCC (India) Ltd* (2020) 20 SCC 760: a party with an interest in the outcome cannot unilaterally appoint a sole arbitrator.
18. *Central Organisation for Railway Electrification v ECI SPIC SMO MCML (JV)* (Constitution Bench, 8 November 2024): unilateral appointment clauses violate equal treatment of parties; check the citation in the reported version.
