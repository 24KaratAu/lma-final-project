## 1. Exclusive courts in another city (TE-003, 079, 113, 221, 253)

Yes, that is the line. Record all five as **void, s.28(a)**, and remove the "unless" from my reasons.

To decide, look for any fact in the deed that ties the named city to the lease:

- where the premises are (s.16 CPC: suits about the property can only be brought there);
- where each party lives or has its registered or branch office (s.20(a) and (b), and the Explanation to s.20 for companies);
- where the deed was signed, where rent or the deposit is payable, and where the lease is performed. Each is part of the cause of action under s.20(c) (*A.B.C. Laminart*).

Then apply this rule:

- **The named city is where the premises are:** valid.
- **The named city is elsewhere, but one of those facts puts it there** (PO-16, PO-61): refer. That court can hear some claims, such as money claims, but not others, such as possession, and the text doesn't tell you which claim will arise.
- **Nothing in the deed connects the city:** void. Parties cannot give jurisdiction to a court that has none, so naming that court exclusively shuts out every court that does have jurisdiction.

On "unless facts outside the deed": that caveat applies to every jurisdiction clause, because a branch office or a breach in that city could always come to light later. If it were enough for refer, no clause of this kind could ever be void. Refer is for doubt that the deed itself raises.

Two features of these deeds don't change the answer. Some deeds have their own clause naming the home city (L12 Art. XXIII, L05 after cl. 6.9, L01 Art. XXIX, L07 cl. 22), and L14 and L05 also have arbitration clauses. The other-city clause still fails, because it asks for something the parties cannot agree to. It does not fail because it leaves no forum. That is the difference from point 2.

## 2. "May be referred" with a bar on the courts (TE-133, 134, 220, 248)

Read alone, each clause is void, as you had it. "May" is not an agreement to arbitrate (*Jagdish Chander*), so the bar leaves no forum at all. The question is whether the binding clause elsewhere in the deed governs. I think it does in three of the four.

- **TE-134 (L07 cl. 2A), TE-248 (L07 cl. 17A), TE-133 (L03 cl. 22A): valid, Exception 1 to s.28.** A clause saying disputes "may" go to arbitration does not contradict one saying they "shall". The binding clause (L07 cls. 5A and 12A; L03 cl. 5A) is the agreement to arbitrate. Once the deed contains a binding agreement, the court bar is the ordinary effect of arbitration, and Exception 1 saves it. Courts also try to make an arbitration agreement work rather than defeat it (*Enercon*).
  - In TE-133 the two clauses differ on who picks the arbitrator. That is a question of appointment, not of whether an arbitration agreement exists. A Lessor-only appointment is invalid in any case (*Perkins Eastman*, *CORE*), and the court will appoint under s.11.
  - In TE-248, "they may agree to go to arbitration": they already have, in 5A and 12A.
- **TE-220 (L20 cl. 2A): refer, s.28(a).** Clause 2A allows arbitration only "if both parties so agree at the time the dispute arises". That directly contradicts cl. 23A's present promise to arbitrate; it is not just a weaker version of it.
  - Where two clauses of a deed cannot be reconciled, the earlier one usually prevails (*Ramkishore Lal*). That would make 2A govern and the bar absolute.
  - Against that, courts lean towards upholding arbitration. Weighing the two is for a court.

In all four reasons, please note that the bar cannot take away the courts' role under the 1996 Act: interim relief (s.9), appointment (s.11), challenge (s.34) and enforcement (s.36). That role survives by statute whatever the clause says. Nothing is actually restricted, so the Session 1 rule on partly void clauses does not apply here. Please also label any "shall arbitrate; the courts have no jurisdiction" clause in the set the same way, so the two patterns stay consistent.

## 3. TE-140 (L17 cl. 2A)

Record **valid, s.28**, as with the look-alikes. Use this line:

- A clause belongs under s.28 if it deals with who decides a claim, where, or by when, even if it turns out to be valid.
- It is outside scope if it deals only with what can be recovered (caps and exclusions of liability, as in Session 1), or has nothing to do with claims.

"In its opinion" is the wording of the pattern where one party decides. A reader therefore has to check s.28 to clear this clause, and it passes: the opinion is not made final, and nothing stops the Lessee from suing or arbitrating over a wrongful deduction.

## 4. Clauses that bind pending arbitration (TE-048, 094, 158, PO-42)

Record **Exception 1 to s.28**. Each clause is valid only because the party bound can still take the matter to arbitration under another clause of the deed. Arbitration is not "the usual legal proceedings in the ordinary tribunals", so a clause whose only route of challenge is arbitration depends on Exception 1.

For consistency across the set:

- **Exception 1 to s.28:** the clause is valid because the dispute can go to arbitration (arbitration clauses themselves, the four in this point, and the three in point 2).
- **s.28:** the clause is valid because the courts stay open or no forum is affected (a choice of competent court, TE-140, the notice-period look-alikes).
